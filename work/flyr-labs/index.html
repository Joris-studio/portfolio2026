Hands-on: making complex flight filters readable and editable

Airlines keep adjusting prices and seat availability per flight, and revenue teams analyse thousands of flights to decide where to act. FLYR builds AI-driven revenue management software for them.

This project redesigned how analysts filter down to the flights that matter: a query editor for selecting flights by combining conditions with AND, OR, NOT and groups.

A list of filters could no longer express the selection

The original interface split filtering in two: a drawer on the right to choose and apply filters, and a strip above the table showing what was active.

A flat list could add filters, but had no way to express alternatives, exclusions or groups.

A list: every filter adds to the selection

Keeping everything in the drawer made the logic harder to follow

One explored direction kept the applied conditions, the operators and groups, and the filter catalogue in one narrow column. Conditions stacked, joined by Not or And; a shaded block grouped conditions that belonged together.

The query had to be read top to bottom, between controls and the filter catalogue.

One of three explored drawer designs. Its bottom actions are placeholders.

Separate choosing filters from composing the query

The drawer stays the place to find and pick filters. The query itself moves out of the drawer into its own editor above the results, where there is room to read and combine the conditions with And, Or, Not and groups.

Give editing space, then return attention to the results

A query needs room while it changes, and very little once it is applied. The filter bar expands for editing and collapses to one line for reviewing.

Examples of each state from different screens, not one sequence.

What I would carry into the next complex tool

  1. 01

    Give each task its own place

    Picking filters and composing their logic are different jobs. Separating them made both clearer.

  2. 02

    Design for the edit and for the glance

    A query needs space while it changes, and very little once it is applied.

  3. 03

    Keep the parts consistent

    Chips that look alike but each have a clear role keep long queries scannable.

One thing from research: users tended to shape their work to what the tool offered and expected little ease of use, which made it harder to uncover what could be better.
