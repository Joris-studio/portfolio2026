From separate features to one user journey

Several development teams, the design team and external partners were building a deposit return app under time pressure. Work started from features. By bringing the existing screens together around the user’s goal, the teams could see what was missing, discuss dependencies and decide what the first version needed.

My part

Together with the design team I collected the existing screens and ordered them around the user’s goal. I then presented that overview, first to key stakeholders and then to a wider group.

Built feature by feature

The client steered on the features needed for launch. Work started from features and back-end functionality, which were then worked out in the app and its screens. Content, design and APIs each had their own workstream.

Each team delivered its part. What was missing was a shared view of the complete user journey: how the parts would work together for someone using the app.

Putting it together raised earlier questions

Once the parts came together, questions surfaced that could not be answered at the end of the chain. Was there an overview of the journey? Did the screens fit together? Did the systems connect?

Each of those questions pointed back to insights and choices earlier in the chain, and to one question for the whole team: what is needed for launch, and what can wait?

Back to the user’s goal

I used the original brief to define the user’s goal, returning packaging and receiving the deposit, and the recurring journey the app was meant to support. With the design team I ordered the existing screens around that goal.

The features did not disappear. They got a place in the journey, under the steps they contribute to. Screens, functionality and systems became equal contributions to the same journey, informing each other instead of handing over in one direction.

What it changed

The overview made visible what was still missing, and that we were behind on what launch required. From there we could discuss specific journeys and the contributions each one needed.

It gave the teams a shared basis to decide what was essential for the first version and what could be deferred.

What this case shows, and what it doesn’t

Shown

How a shared view of the user journey helped teams discuss dependencies and the scope of the first version.

Not claimed

That a new formal development process was introduced. The journey was a shared frame for decisions, not a new way of working on paper.
